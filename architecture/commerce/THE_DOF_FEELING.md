# DOF Buyer Experience — THE_DOF_FEELING

**Status:** For Founder approval (design only) · v1.0 · 2026-07-27
The definition document: what buying on DOF feels like, named precisely enough that every future increment can be tested against it.

---

## 1. The feeling, named

**DOF feels like being a regular at a market stall — kept alive between visits.**

Unpacked into its three components:

1. **Presence** — there is a person here, working, *today*. (The street's liveness; the presence line; the workshop wait.)
2. **Witness** — I saw the thing become, I saw the promise kept, and the record remembers with me. (Sparks during the wait; the promise ledger; the biography.)
3. **Regularhood** — I am recognized because of what I chose to do here, and returning feels like returning. (The corner; follows; "you've bought here before.")

Every buyer-facing decision from C3 onward can be tested in one line: *does this make DOF feel more like a stall street, or more like a website?*

## 2. The feeling, contrasted

- Amazon feels like **infrastructure**: magnificent, invisible, nobody's hands anywhere. Buying is logistics.
- Etsy feels like **a bazaar with a ratings PA system**: makers present but shouting numbers at you.
- Shopify feels like **a thousand isolated boutiques**: identity without fabric; every door a fresh act of faith.
- **DOF feels like market morning**: many stalls, one street, today's word, your regular corners — and the street itself quietly guarantees the handshake (the keystone).

## 3. The vocabulary of the feeling (words the product uses, words it never uses)

**Uses:** maker, shop, street, shelf, promise, "on its way to being yours," "from Rosa's hands," today, this week.
**Never uses:** vendor, SKU, marketplace listing, ETA, logistics, "your package," conversion, deal-of-the-day urgency, "customers also bought."
The vocabulary IS the feeling at the sentence level — this extends the merchant-language law to the buyer side, and lint-level copy review at each increment enforces it.

## 4. The restraint clause

The feeling is produced by **real events rendered warmly, and by nothing else**. The moment the platform decorates (confetti, fake handwriting, rustic costume), the stall becomes a theme park of itself. Every emotional surface in this design traces to: a spark someone wrote, a photo someone took, a promise someone kept, a return someone chose. That traceability is the whole trick — and it is why none of this ages into cringe: real things don't.

## 5. The final test (answered)

After this design, does someone say *"I bought a product"* or *"I discovered a maker"*?

The journey is built so the sentence mutates as it proceeds: at the stall — *"found this knitter"*; during the wait — *"she's making it this week"*; at arrival — *"she got it to me early"*; a year on — *"this is the blanket from that knitter on DOF — look, I still have the story."*

Not one of those sentences is "I bought a product." The subject of every sentence is the maker. **That grammatical fact is the design's proof: DOF's buying journey keeps the maker as the subject of the buyer's story, from first click to next year.**
