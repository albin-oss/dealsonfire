# DOF Merchant Experience — MERCHANT_LONG_TERM_RELATIONSHIP

**Status:** For Founder approval (design only) · v1.0 · 2026-07-27
Six months, two years, ten years: how the relationship compounds without curdling into dependency, and how it ends with dignity if it ends. The copyability challenge applies hardest here — retention is where incumbents deploy their darkest patterns; DOF's long game is built from the opposite material.

---

## 1. What compounds (the assets only time can build)

- **The record** — promise-kept counts, repeat buyers, refund fairness — grows monotonically more valuable and cannot be bought, transferred, or faked. At year two it is the merchant's most defensible business asset anywhere on the internet: portable proof of being good at keeping promises. (It is *theirs*: rendered from their events, visible to their buyers, exportable — §4.)
- **The moments** — the Moment Ledger becomes a founding-story archive: first sale, first kept promise, first return buyer, hundredth order, each with its date. Makers retell origin stories forever; DOF is the only platform that *keeps* them.
- **The shelf of made things** (C5+ seam, composition): the merchant's view over completed orders — every thing that left the bench, when, to which promise. The maker's own biography-of-things, mirror of the buyer keepsake. A woodworker's "everything I've made this year" is pride no dashboard ever produced.
- **Regulars** — followers who buy again, corner-keepers who return. The street's social fabric ages into the merchant's *clientele* — the thing family businesses actually mean by "the business."

## 2. Growth without console-ification
Scale changes surface depth, never nature (the frozen Surface-Level law, ADR-006): the four-product knitter and the forty-product roaster run the same workshop at different depths — more bench, same chairs. The rhythm test (DAILY_RHYTHM §8) binds at every scale; if the hundredth-order shop needs a different *kind* of software, the design has failed, not the merchant.

## 3. The seasons of a shop (honesty about the long middle)
Year two is mostly ordinary weeks. The relationship holds because ordinary weeks are *complete* on DOF: the morning visit, an order or none, a spark when something happened. The platform's job in the long middle is to stay light and keep the record safe — not to invent excitement. **Named seam (gated):** the yearly note — one merchant-private page each January: "your year on the street," counts and moments only, no comparisons, no rankings, shareable only by the merchant's choice. Composition over existing events; founder gate before it exists (it is an observable surface and a tone risk if done wrong — greeting-card danger).

## 4. Dignity at the exits
The relationship's honesty is proven at its endings:
- **Pausing** (the resting stall, DAILY_RHYTHM §6): rest without penalty or nag.
- **Leaving:** the merchant's record, story, moments, and order history export cleanly (their data, their work); the storefront closes without dark-pattern retention ("are you SURE? your followers will miss you!" — banned). Open orders must resolve first (the closure policy already frozen in CONNECT_FUNDS_FLOW §5) — promises outrank departure, which is itself the brand.
- **Returning:** a closed shop that reopens finds its record intact — the street remembers work, not absence.

## 5. The copyability answer
Shopify's long game is app-store lock-in; Etsy's is algorithmic dependency; Amazon's is operational capture. **DOF's long game is possession**: after two years a merchant owns a record, a clientele, and an archive that are worth more *because* the platform never held them hostage. The retention mechanism is that leaving would mean leaving something genuinely theirs — which is exactly why they won't. None of the three incumbents can copy this without dismantling their own retention economics.

## 6. The six-month answer (the founder's final test)
Six months in, the workspace contains: their moments with dates · their record in counts · their regulars · their shelf of made things · tomorrow's one thing. Every object in the room is something they did; the street outside the door is live with people they know. The sentence that describes this is not "I manage an online store." It is — verbatim from the design — **"I have my own workshop on a living street."** The test passes not by copywriting but by inventory: there is nothing in the room *to manage* except the work itself.
